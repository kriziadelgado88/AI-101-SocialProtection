The three ingredients of AI

Every AI system rests on three things working together, often called the AI triad. Take any one away and it does not work. Tap each one to see why.

The AI triad

All three, together

A great method with no data learns nothing. Mountains of data with no compute cannot be trained. The three only produce a working system when they come together.

Tap data, compute, or algorithms in the triangle.

Why this matters for you. When you weigh any AI proposal, ask about all three. Is the data accurate, fair, and lawful to use? Is there enough compute, and at what cost? Is the method proven for this task? A weak link in any one of them puts the whole project at risk.

How we got here

AI is not new. The idea is over seventy years old. What changed recently is the raw computing power and data behind it. Here are the moments that matter.

1947

The transistor is invented

At Bell Labs, a tiny electronic switch is born. Every computer chip since is built from these switches. This is the seed of all modern computing.

1950

Turing asks if machines can think

Alan Turing proposes a simple test: can a machine hold a conversation well enough to seem human? The question still frames the field.

1956

The term "artificial intelligence" is coined

A summer workshop at Dartmouth College gives the field its name and launches it as a research area.

1965

Moore's Law is observed

Gordon Moore notices that the number of transistors on a chip keeps doubling. Computing gets cheaper and faster for decades, which later becomes the fuel for AI.

1970s & 1980s

The "AI winters"

Progress stalls and funding dries up twice. The ambitions of the time run far ahead of the available computing power.

1997

A computer beats the world chess champion

IBM's Deep Blue defeats Garry Kasparov, a public sign that machines can outmatch people at narrow, rule-bound tasks.

2012

Deep learning breaks through

A neural network called AlexNet wins an image-recognition contest by a wide margin, powered by graphics chips. The modern AI boom begins.

2017

The Transformer arrives

A new design for handling language, introduced in a paper titled "Attention Is All You Need," becomes the engine inside today's chatbots.

2020

Scaling laws and GPT-3

Researchers show that bigger models trained on more data and compute get predictably better. Large language models start to feel genuinely capable.

2022

ChatGPT reaches everyone

A free chatbot puts generative AI in the hands of the public. Adoption is the fastest of any consumer tool in history.

2023 onward

Governments respond

Generative AI spreads into daily work while regulators move to set rules, such as the European Union's AI Act, the first broad law of its kind.

Why hardware decides what AI can do

AI runs on physical machines. Two ideas explain why it suddenly took off: the transistor that made cheap computing possible, and the scaling laws that showed how to turn that computing into capability.

🔌 The transistor

A transistor is a microscopic switch that flips between on and off, the 1s and 0s a computer thinks in. The first one in 1947 was the size of a hand. Today billions fit on a chip smaller than a fingernail.

📈 Moore's Law

For decades the number of transistors per chip doubled roughly every two years. That steady doubling made computing power cheap and abundant, which is the raw material every AI system consumes.

0 · off
Tap the switch. A transistor is just this, an on-off switch, built billions of times onto one chip.
The link to AI. Modern AI needs vast amounts of computing power, called compute. Graphics chips first built for video games turned out to be ideal for it, because they run many small calculations at once. Without cheap, plentiful compute, today's AI would not exist.

The scaling laws (Kaplan, 2020)

In 2020 a team led by Jared Kaplan found something striking. As you increase three things, a model's performance improves in a smooth, predictable curve. The three things are model size (parameters), training data, and compute. Drag the slider to feel how it behaves.

Why officials should care. Predictability changed the game. Once teams knew that more compute would reliably buy more capability, a race to build larger models began. A later refinement in 2022, known as Chinchilla, showed that data should grow alongside model size for the best results.
The catch. Bigger is not free. Cost and energy climb steeply, the gains get smaller as you scale, and scale alone does not remove bias or stop a model from being confidently wrong. Capability and trustworthiness are separate problems.

The Transformer is the design inside today's chatbots, including ChatGPT and Claude. It is the T in GPT. Its one big trick is called attention.

Attention means that for every word, the model looks at all the other words and decides which ones help it make sense. Tap any word below to see what it leans on.

Agentic AI: from answering to doing

So far the AI you have seen produces content. You ask, it answers. Agentic AI is the next step. You hand it a goal, and it carries out the steps to reach it.

💬 A chatbot

You ask a question. It gives an answer and stops. The doing is left to you.

🤖 An agent

You set a goal. It plans the steps, uses tools to carry them out, checks its progress, and reports back when finished.

What an agent is made of

An agent is the model plus a few parts around it that let it plan, remember, look things up, and act. Here is how they fit together.

🧠 Reasoning core

The model at the center that decides what to do at each step.

🗺️ Planning

Breaks the goal into an ordered set of steps before acting.

💾 Memory

Keeps track of what has happened and what it learned, within a task and sometimes across tasks.

📚 Knowledge (RAG)

Looks up trusted information from a connected source before it answers.

🛠️ Tools

The outside actions it can take, such as search, a calculation, or updating a record.

🛡️ Guardrails

The safety limits around everything, setting what it may and may not do.

A social-protection example. A citizen asks about their application. A benefits assistant agent looks up the case, checks which documents are missing, and drafts a reminder to the applicant. It then hands the draft to a caseworker to review, approve, and send. The agent does the legwork. The person keeps the decision.

Why agents need tighter limits. An agent does things, so a mistake has direct effects. Never let it take an irreversible action on its own, such as stopping a payment, denying a claim, or sending an official letter. Require human approval before anything that affects a person, keep a full log of what it did, and start at the suggest-only level.

What AI cannot do, and common myths

A balanced 101 needs the limits too. These are the misunderstandings worth clearing up before anyone designs a program.

Myth: AI thinks and understands like a person.
Reality. It finds patterns in data and predicts likely outputs. There is no awareness or understanding behind it.
Myth: AI is objective and neutral.
Reality. It learns from human data, so it can absorb and repeat human bias. Fairness has to be designed in and tested for.
Myth: if the computer says it, it must be right.
Reality. Models can state false things with full confidence. Outputs need checking, above all for facts and for people's entitlements.
Myth: bigger models are always better.
Reality. More scale helps, yet it raises cost and energy and does not remove bias or error. Capability and trustworthiness are separate things.
Myth: AI can replace human judgment about people.
Reality. For any decision that affects someone's support, it should assist while a person stays in charge.
Myth: a model knows everything up to today.
Reality. It only knows what was in its training unless it is connected to live information, so it can be out of date.
The honest summary. AI is a powerful pattern tool. It can be confidently wrong, it can carry the biases in its data, and it does not know anything that was not in its training unless it is connected to live information. Treat its output as a draft to check, not a verdict.

What this means for social protection

AI can strengthen safety nets, and it can also harm the people they exist to protect. Both are real. Officials need to hold the opportunity and the risk in view at the same time.

Where it can help

💬 Faster service

Chatbots and document readers can answer benefit questions around the clock and speed up application handling, freeing staff for complex cases.

🔎 Better take-up

Models can help find people who qualify for support but never applied, so programs reach more of the people they are meant to serve.

🌦️ Early warning

Forecasts can flag droughts, price shocks, or demand spikes, letting governments trigger support before a crisis deepens.

🗂️ Caseworker support

AI can summarize long case files and translate across languages, giving frontline staff more time with people instead of paperwork.

Where it can go wrong

The Netherlands, childcare benefits. A self-learning fraud-detection system wrongly labelled tens of thousands of families as fraudsters, with people of immigrant background hit hardest. Families were pushed into severe debt, and the national government resigned in 2021. The system was opaque and lacked proper human review.
Australia, "Robodebt." An automated system raised false debts against hundreds of thousands of people by matching data crudely and removing human checks. It was ruled unlawful and caused deep and lasting harm.

The pattern in both is the same: a poorly tested model, little transparency, and no human truly in control. At the scale of a national program, a small error rate becomes harm to thousands of real people.

The safeguards that matter most. Keep a human in charge of any decision that can deny someone support. Give people a clear way to ask why and to appeal. Test for bias before and during use. Tell people when AI is involved. Protect sensitive data. Start with small, low-stakes pilots and watch them closely.

A framework for your own solutions

A simple, repeatable way to weigh any idea before you commit to it.

Name the real problem

State the citizen or system pain you want to fix, in one sentence, before naming any technology.

Check that AI fits

Is there a genuine pattern in data to learn from? Sometimes a clearer form or a rule change solves it without AI.

Weigh the stakes

If a mistake could deny someone food, shelter, or income, keep a person making the final call.

Get the data right

Ask if the data is accurate, representative of everyone served, and lawful and ethical to use.

Build in oversight

Design human review, a transparent explanation, and a working appeal route from day one.

Pilot, measure, then scale

Start small, compare results against the old way, and check for unequal effects on different groups.

Set clear accountability

Decide in advance who is answerable when the system makes a mistake, and how harm gets put right.
